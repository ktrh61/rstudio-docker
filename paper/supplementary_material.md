## Supplementary Methods

### Data sources and expression matrix

We queried the National Cancer Institute (NCI) Genomic Data Commons (GDC) API for open-access files in project REBC-THYR under GDC Data Release 45.0 <!-- N-92 -->, using data category Transcriptome Profiling, experimental strategy RNA-Seq, data type Gene Expression Quantification, and workflow type STAR - Counts. The query returned 906 released files <!-- N-89 -->. The accompanying manifest contained the file UUIDs, names, sizes, and GDC-provided MD5 checksums (manifest MD5, 7defb0c5574453474c67dfac8367a589) <!-- N-89 -->; we verified every downloaded file against its recorded checksum.

Clinical data came from Data S1 of Morton et al. (2021), which we read with all columns retained and without editing any values. We converted missing markers to NA and typed a column as numeric only when every non-missing value parsed as numeric.

We mapped files to cases and biospecimens through the GDC API using GenomicDataCommons (Morgan and Davis 2025) and assembled one count assay per sample in a SummarizedExperiment container (Morgan et al. 2025). Removing all-zero genes yielded 58,448 genes and 906 samples <!-- N-14 -->. We derived exon-union gene lengths from the GDC GENCODE v36 reference annotation using rtracklayer (Lawrence et al. 2009) <!-- N-66 -->.

For each sample, we determined library strandedness from the totals of the two STAR stranded columns. A library was classified as stranded when the smaller total was no more than half the larger, in which case the larger column was used; otherwise the unstranded column was used <!-- N-67 -->. All 906 libraries were classified as reverse stranded; their smaller-to-larger ratios ranged from 0.056 to 0.110, well below the 0.5 threshold <!-- N-77 -->. GDC TPM and FPKM columns were not used. For the REO analysis, we recalculated TPM from the selected count assay and exon-union gene lengths.

### Exposure metric: Assigned Share

We calculated Assigned Share associated with the expected value of the excess relative risk using NIH IREP version 5.7.3 (Kocher et al. 2008). The fixed inputs were electrons E>15 keV, acute exposure rate, organ dose entered as a constant in cSv=mGy/10, recorded sex, exposure year 1986, birth year calculated as 1986 minus age at exposure, and surgery year calculated as birth year plus age at surgery. All other settings remained at the program defaults: user-defined uncertainty distribution Lognormal(1,1), 10,000 iterations, and random-number seed 99 <!-- N-68 -->. We checked the dose and age inputs in the versioned AS file case by case against the clinical table.

IREP guidance identifies iodine-131 as an example of the electrons E>15 keV route. This route has a radiation effectiveness factor of 1.0 relative to reference higher-energy photons (Land et al. 2003) <!-- N-83 -->, with independent microdosimetric support for similar biological effectiveness of internal iodine-131 and external gamma rays per absorbed dose (Sato et al. 2014). This addresses radiation quality, not the transfer of a thyroid risk model fitted to external photon exposures. The IREP thyroid model is based on atomic-bomb survivors and populations irradiated as children for medical indications (Ron et al. 1995; Kocher et al. 2008).

We retained the acute setting to preserve computational continuity with earlier IREP applications to post-Chernobyl papillary carcinoma from the same research program (Zurnadzhy et al. 2022; Bogdanova et al. 2022). These studies document the provenance of the convention rather than independently validating acute coding for protracted internal iodine-131 exposure. In IREP, exposure rate affects the calculation through the dose and dose-rate effectiveness factor. Under the acute setting the factor approaches 1 at and above an uncertain reference dose distributed log-uniformly from 0.03 to 0.2 Gy <!-- N-83 -->; the minimum dose in the High-AS band was 188 mGy <!-- N-84 -->. A chronic calculation lowers the numeric AS scale.

Dose was entered as a point value, and dosimetric or risk-model uncertainty was not propagated into band membership. Dose-zero cases formed the separate reference group. Exposed cases were classified as Low (0<AS<33.3), Mid (33.3≤AS<66.6), and High (AS≥66.6); no case lay on a boundary <!-- N-69 -->. The cut-points were operational boundaries on the prespecified acute-calculation scale and were not interpreted as biological or etiological probability thresholds. The composition of all 440 cases by analysis stratum, band, and pairing status is in Table S1 <!-- N-11 -->. AS values were available for every exposed case in the two analysis strata (57 RET fusion-positive and 142 BRAF V600E cases) <!-- N-93 -->. Cases outside the strata entered no analysis; the versioned IREP input file also carries values for 14 exposed cases outside the strata, which were not used <!-- N-93 -->.

### Quality control and analysis cohorts

Within each observed group-by-tissue submatrix, we filtered genes with edgeR's filterByExpr (Chen et al. 2016; Chen et al. 2025) before applying principal-component-scores-based outlier detection (PC-OD). We retained the pre-filter library sizes as the counts-per-million denominators and used unnormalized log counts per million with prior count 2 so that composition anomalies were not first attenuated by between-sample normalization. At each iteration, the most extreme sample on the first principal component was removed using the two-sided PC-OD rule at α=0.05, and the procedure continued until no further sample was rejected (Nakayama et al. 2024) <!-- N-78 -->. PC-OD was a QC and cohort-definition step, not a gene-level hypothesis test, and its finalized flags were held fixed in downstream inference. It flagged no tumor or normal sample in the RET fusion-positive stratum; its only flag among the four main target groups was one High-AS BRAF V600E-positive tumor <!-- N-87 -->. Thus, it did not alter the primary RET fusion-positive cohort.

We estimated relative tumor purity jointly for dose-zero and High-AS cases within each driver cohort using an in-house implementation of the initial matched-pair proportion score from contamDE-lm rather than its iterative pseudo-likelihood differential-expression model (Shen et al. 2016; Ji et al. 2020). Protein-coding, filterByExpr-reduced paired counts were MUREN-normalized (Feng and Li 2021). A paired limma-voom model with robust empirical Bayes selected the up- and down-regulated working genes used to construct the score (Ritchie et al. 2015), which was rescaled so that the cohort maximum was 1. The limma-voom fit served only this purity-estimation step and was not used for the reported gene-level inference. Joint estimation placed both groups on a common relative scale; this assumes a common, driver-dominated tumor reference within each driver cohort. We treated purity as a relative score within the jointly estimated cases, not as an absolute cellular fraction or a device for separating purity from all AS-aligned expression structure.

Case pairing used the GDC merged-aliquot expression assays. Each included case was required to have one unique merged tumor and one unique merged normal sample. The main cohort additionally required RET fusion-positive classification or BRAF V600E-positive classification without another candidate driver mutation in the source cohort's WGS or RNA calls (175 of 190 V600E cases <!-- N-94 -->), membership in the dose-zero or High-AS group, both tissues passing the outlier screen, and relative purity at least 0.6.

The REO construction set comprised the RET fusion-positive subset of the main cohort. The intermediate-band application set comprised RET fusion-positive PTCs from the Low-AS and Mid-AS bands, whether or not a matched normal was available. Construction-cohort outlier and purity exclusions were not applied to this set; corresponding metrics were reported as ancillary diagnostics and did not authorize exclusions.

### Analysis contrasts

The four High-AS versus dose-zero contrasts were RET-tumor (`R_Tumor` in the analysis files), RET-normal (`R_Normal`), BRAF-tumor (`B_Tumor`), and BRAF-normal (`B_Normal`). These labels refer to contrasts, not sample groups. In each contrast, group X was dose-zero and group Y was High-AS. The Brunner–Munzel relative effect (probabilistic index; Brunner and Munzel 2000) was denoted θ:

θ = Pr(X<Y) + 0.5 Pr(X=Y).

Values of θ>0.5 indicated that expression tended to be higher in High-AS cases. The signed effect shown in Figure 2 was 2θ−1. Higher Criticism (Donoho and Jin 2004) was the single primary contrast-level test for RET-tumor. The corresponding tests in the other three contrasts addressed distinct secondary questions. We reported all four omnibus p-values separately and did not combine them into a study-level decision. Accordingly, no across-contrast multiplicity adjustment was applied, and we made no claim of study-wide FDR or across-contrast family-wise error control.

### Covariate disclosure and estimand

For exposed cases, age at exposure entered the AS calculation as a radiation-risk modifier. Dose-zero cases had no corresponding age at exposure, so this variable was not compared between groups. We characterized the observable age structure in each driver stratum using the Hodges–Lehmann median difference (Hodges and Lehmann 1963) in age at surgery and the Brunner–Munzel relative effect defined above. These complementary rank-based summaries described group separation on the original scale (years) and on a probability scale without imposing a normal model. We obtained 95% percentile bootstrap intervals (Efron and Tibshirani 1993) for both estimates from 9,999 resamples drawn separately within each group (seed 19450809) <!-- N-63 -->. No p-value was calculated; these summaries characterized group structure rather than testing confounding or demonstrating balance.

We reported sex by group because expression of sex-chromosome genes can differ with group composition. Using GENCODE v36 chromosome assignments, we counted X- and Y-linked genes among all genes tested in each contrast and among the genes meeting the Storey q<0.10 rule, with the latter also separated by effect direction; no chromosome-enrichment test was performed. In the finalized IREP calculations, the AS values were numerically insensitive to the recorded sex input; sex was therefore not regarded as absorbed into the exposure metric. RET fusion-partner counts were reported by band; the BRAF stratum contained BRAF V600E by construction. We fitted no common covariate-adjusted model: conditioning on the age inputs used to construct AS would change the marginal AS-band estimand, relative purity was expression-derived and used for eligibility, and fusion partner may be part of the biological pathway under study. Treating them collectively as nuisance terms would not, by itself, identify a radiation-specific effect.

The finalized analysis objects contained no sequencing-batch, processing-batch, center, or collection-period field suitable for a group-balance calculation. This unavailable information is treated as an unassessed source of technical confounding. Available relative-purity, age, sex, and fusion-partner variables were reported descriptively without an additional hypothesis test.

### Normalization

For each contrast, we restricted counts to protein-coding genes and applied edgeR's filterByExpr again, using the two contrast groups, before normalization with an in-house three-iteration implementation of DEGES-MUREN (Kadota et al. 2012; Sun et al. 2013; Feng and Li 2021). In this two-group use, filterByExpr uses the group sizes to set the required number of expressed samples but does not use the identities of the samples meeting that expression rule; for fixed group sizes, the retained set is therefore invariant to relabeling. The MUREN step used its single-parameter pairwise least-trimmed-squares form, implemented with `MASS::ltsreg` (Venables and Ripley 2002), the engine used by the original MUREN implementation. At each iteration, a Brunner–Munzel permutation screen enumerated all label allocations and produced Storey q-values (Storey 2002) with λ=0.5; this screen replaced the model-based differential-expression screen in the original DEGES scheme. The larger of the q<0.10 set and the top 5% of genes by raw p-value was removed from scaling-factor estimation, implementing the floorPDEG guard <!-- N-71 -->. Tested-gene counts, screen π0 estimates, Jaccard convergence, and scaling-factor ranges are in Table S2 <!-- N-15 -->.

Because the DEGES exclusion screen used the observed group labels, the gene-level exhaustive enumeration was conditional on the resulting normalized matrix.

### Gene-level differential expression

For each gene, we evaluated the studentized Brunner–Munzel statistic (two-sided; Neubert and Brunner 2007) for every allocation of nx labels among n samples on the finalized normalized matrix. No count-distribution model or asymptotic p-value approximation was used. Exhaustive enumeration removed Monte Carlo error and the 1/(B+1) resolution floor (Phipson and Smyth 2010); inference remained conditional on exchangeability of the retained samples. Ties were retained in the effect estimate and statistic.

Gene-level p-values were converted to Storey q-values with the plug-in estimate of π0 at a fixed λ=0.5 and a threshold of q<0.10 <!-- N-05 -->. We fixed λ before analysis rather than selecting it adaptively from strongly dependent, co-expressed tests; 0.5 was also the fixed value used throughout the calculations in Storey (2002). This choice was compatible with the working hypothesis of weak expression differences distributed across many genes, but it was not treated as proof of FDR control under arbitrary gene dependence. We therefore refer to “genes meeting the prespecified Storey q rule” and reserve the contrast-level existence statement for the Higher Criticism label-permutation result. Complete per-gene results for every tested gene in each contrast are provided in Supplementary Data 2.

### Contrast-level omnibus inference

Higher Criticism was the prespecified primary omnibus statistic. The scan used empirical per-gene p-values referred to the pooled distribution of all genes' shuffle statistics, excluded p-values at or below 1/n (the HC+ form), and covered the smallest α0=0.1 fraction of the ordered p-values. It was selected to aggregate moderately small ordered p-values rather than make the contrast-level conclusion depend on one extreme gene or a fixed gene-count cutoff. Its p-value was calculated with plus-one counting against the contrast's own label-shuffle distribution, so the analytic independence and sparsity assumptions of the original Higher Criticism null were not used. The RET-tumor Higher Criticism test was the single primary contrast-level test; the corresponding statistics in the other contrasts answered separate secondary questions. Count statistics at fixed cutoffs and the maximum statistic were retained as descriptive rows, calibrated against the same shuffles with plus-one counting. The full rejection curve R(α) was retained to show how the displayed gene count varied across q thresholds.

Exhaustive recomputation of the genome-wide omnibus profiles over every label allocation was not computationally practical. For each contrast, we therefore generated and stored 9,999 label shuffles using seed 19860426 <!-- N-04 -->. Shuffling broke the association between group labels and expression while retaining each sample's joint expression profile and hence the dependence among genes. These shuffles provided the null distributions for the omnibus statistics and the plug-in π0 estimator and were reused unchanged for gene-set inference.

Because we defined no binary contrast label, we report the omnibus p-values, per-gene q-values, and rejection curves as continuous evidence. We do not use “support” or “no support” wording to imply an undeclared cutoff.

### Gene-set inference

We used the complete gene ranking without a DEG-list threshold. We ranked genes by tie-averaged normal scores of the signed Brunner–Munzel statistic and evaluated the in-house weighted running-sum statistic with gseaParam=1 only at tie-block boundaries. Automated tests verified equality to the standard GSEA statistic for tie-free input (Subramanian et al. 2005). For each set, we obtained its normalized enrichment score (NES) by dividing the observed enrichment score by the mean absolute enrichment score among null values of the same sign; the same sign-specific divisor was applied to that set's null scores.

The gene-set null comprised these same 9,999 saved label shuffles for each contrast. Permuting sample labels rather than genes preserved the subject-level gene-dependence structure, and reusing the shuffle bank ensured that the omnibus and gene-set procedures were evaluated on the same label allocations. For an observed positive NES, the sign-conditional permutation p-value was (1 plus the number of null NES values at least as large as observed)/(1 plus the number of non-negative null NES values); the corresponding lower-tail counts were used for a negative NES. We applied the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure within each collection (Benjamini and Hochberg 1995), using q<0.10 without making a cross-collection claim. We did not estimate π0 at the set level; in this setting, the BH procedure is equivalent to setting π0=1. This choice avoided reliance on a potentially unstable plug-in estimate from the dependent set-level p-values.

We obtained the gene-set definitions with msigdbr 26.1.0 (Dolgalev 2026). Before the 15–500-gene size filter, the four collections were Hallmark (50 sets; Liberzon et al. 2015); C2 canonical pathways restricted to Reactome, WikiPathways, KEGG MEDICUS, BioCarta, and PID (3,910 sets; the legacy KEGG subcollection was excluded); C5 GO Biological Process (7,538 sets); and a radiation-curated subset of C2:CGP (28 sets) <!-- N-29 -->. The radiation curation rule was fixed before the reported set-level run <!-- N-55 -->.

For the spike-in, we started from the finalized normalized counts-per-million matrix for the BRAF-tumor contrast and multiplied the values of the 195 HALLMARK_ADIPOGENESIS genes present in the matrix by 1.15 in its nine High-AS samples <!-- N-30 -->. We then repeated the complete ranking and gene-set procedure described above with 9,999 label shuffles and diagnostic seed 19450809. We defined the planted set's within-Hallmark rank by ascending BH q-value. This was a single coherent-signal check, not a general power analysis.

For over-representation analysis, RET-tumor genes meeting Storey q<0.10 were split into higher, lower, and combined lists. One-sided hypergeometric tests used all genes tested in the RET-tumor contrast as the universe, with BH adjustment within each family-by-list combination. For a list of n genes, a set of K genes, and a universe of N genes, the expected overlap under this reference was nK/N. Within a family-by-list cell, the set with the smallest raw hypergeometric p-value was used when identifying a single leading descriptive example; adjusted q-values, not that descriptive ordering, carried the q<0.10 rule. In the terminology of Goeman and Bühlmann (2007), the statistic is competitive, but its gene-sampling p-value does not refer to subject-level label randomization.

### Complete-null assessment of the set-level procedure

Because genes are co-expressed and gene sets overlap, we assessed rather than assumed the complete-null operating characteristics of the set-level procedure. For each contrast, we generated 10,099 label shuffles using seed 19450809, independently of the shuffles used for the reported inference <!-- N-06 -->. The first 9,999 shuffles formed a shared null pool, and the remaining 100 served as held-out pseudo-observations. For each pseudo-observation, we normalized the set-level enrichment scores against the shared pool, calculated sign-conditional permutation p-values, applied the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure within each collection, and recorded discoveries at q<0.10.

For each contrast-by-collection cell, the proportion of pseudo-observations yielding at least one discovery estimated the probability of at least one false discovery under the complete null, where the false discovery rate and family-wise error rate coincide. We also reported the mean number of discoveries. As a descriptive summary only, we pooled the 100 any-discovery indicators from each of the 16 cells and reported their total and proportion. Clopper–Pearson intervals (Clopper and Pearson 1934) were calculated as descriptive reference intervals; because the 100 pseudo-observations shared one null pool, their discovery indicators were dependent, and these intervals do not have exact coverage for the shared-pool design.

Before the reported gene-set analysis, an earlier development assessment using a 999-shuffle reference pool gave pooled complete-null rates of 0.140 and 0.221 for two pooled tail-ratio procedures, with a maximum cell-specific rate of 0.44 <!-- N-58 -->. The per-set permutation procedure with within-collection Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment gave 0.045 and was selected <!-- N-57 -->. The final assessment described above used a 9,999-shuffle reference pool and gave a descriptive pooled rate of 0.064 <!-- N-24 -->; one cell had a rate of 0.18 <!-- N-25 -->. These measurements characterize behavior under the complete null but do not establish false-discovery-rate control under partial alternatives. Cell-level findings are reported in Supplementary Results.

### Between-stratum concordance

For each tissue, we intersected the RET and BRAF profiles of signed gene-level Brunner–Munzel statistics and compared them using Spearman correlation. To construct the null reference, we generated 9,999 label shuffles separately from those used for omnibus and gene-set inference, using a distinct seed for each contrast derived from the base seed 19450809 <!-- N-73 -->. At each iteration, labels were shuffled independently in the RET and BRAF contrasts before their signed-statistic profiles were correlated. The 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles formed the empirical reference interval, and a two-sided Monte Carlo p-value was calculated with plus-one counting. The normal-tissue comparison was prespecified, whereas the tumor-tissue comparison was descriptive. This null represents the case in which neither stratum carries label-aligned structure; a correlation outside the reference interval does not distinguish exposure-associated structure from a covariate shared across strata.

### REO panel construction and intermediate-band application

Following the within-sample gene-ordering framework (Geman et al. 2004; Wang et al. 2015; Guan et al. 2016), we recalculated TPM from the selected count assay using exon-union gene lengths because comparisons across different genes require length normalization. We ranked genes by the absolute distance of the Brunner–Munzel effect from 0.5 and took the top 500 without using the q<0.10 list. We divided these genes into higher-expression (effect >0.5) and lower-expression (effect <0.5) groups and retained a gene for pairing only when its log2 TPM was finite in every dose-zero and High-AS construction sample. We evaluated every eligible higher-by-lower gene pair.

For a gene pair, r was the within-sample log2-TPM difference between its higher- and lower-expression genes. The dead zone was |r|<log2(1.2). We defined the dose-zero reference sign by the majority sign outside the dead zone and allowed at most one opposite-sign dose-zero sample; the 10th percentile of |r| among dose-zero samples also had to be at least log2(1.5). In High-AS samples, the reversal rate was the number outside the dead zone with a sign opposite to the dose-zero reference divided by the total number of High-AS construction samples. A pair qualified when this rate was greater than 50% but less than 100% <!-- N-74 -->. Qualified pairs were ranked by the absolute difference between the dose-zero and High-AS median r values. Greedy selection prohibited gene reuse and excluded a pair whose r profile had a Spearman correlation of at least 0.75 with a retained pair <!-- N-75 -->. The target was 10 pairs <!-- N-37 -->.

The reversal score counted pairs outside the dead zone that opposed the dose-zero reference sign. We set the classification cutoff at the maximum reversal score among dose-zero construction samples; a sample was classified as positive only when its score exceeded that cutoff. We did not change the panel or its classification rule after construction.

We defined binary and graded readouts for the panel. In the dose-zero and High-AS construction bands, the binary readout characterized separation between the groups used to construct the panel. Demonstrating this separation was a prerequisite for application elsewhere, but it was not an unbiased estimate of classification performance because the same cases informed pair selection and the dose-zero cases set the cutoff. We then applied the classification rule unchanged to Low-AS and Mid-AS RET fusion-positive PTCs and treated the resulting classifications as descriptive application results.

We summarized graded scores across all four bands, with dose-zero and High-AS serving as construction anchors and the panel applied without refitting to Low-AS and Mid-AS. Within the two intermediate bands, the prespecified one-sided Brunner–Munzel comparison assessed whether scores tended to be higher in Mid-AS than in Low-AS. Exhaustive enumeration would have required 8,597,496,600 label allocations, so we used 999,999 Monte Carlo allocations with seed 19860426 and plus-one counting <!-- N-91 -->. This comparison did not test linearity, four-band monotonicity, or a dose–response form. Neither it nor the four-band display constituted independent validation because the panel and score direction came from the construction bands.

### Ancillary REO quality and purity diagnostics

As a non-exclusion QC diagnostic, we applied the same PC-OD input construction separately within the Low-AS and Mid-AS RET fusion-positive PTCs. The resulting flags had no exclusion authority; every intermediate-band case remained in the REO application.

For cases with matched normal tissue, we estimated relative purity on a common scale by pooling paired cases across all four RET groups in one contamDE-lm proportion run and summarized the resulting scores in each band by their median. Cases without matched normal tissue remained in the REO application but did not enter the purity analyses.

We summarized associations between relative purity and REO score using Spearman correlations separately within Low-AS and Mid-AS cases and after pooling the two bands. In the pooled intermediate-band cases, we also calculated the band–score and band–purity Spearman correlations. To describe the band–score association after rank-scale adjustment for purity, we calculated the partial Spearman coefficient by correlating residuals from separate linear projections of band rank and score rank on purity rank.

In a separate diagnostic permutation procedure, we permuted the Low-AS and Mid-AS labels 9,999 times using seed 19860426 while retaining each sample's observed score–purity pair. With Mid-AS coded above Low-AS, we calculated an upper-tail p-value for a positive partial coefficient using plus-one counting. Because this scheme also breaks the band–purity association, the resulting p-value was not interpreted as conditional inference at fixed purity.

As a further descriptive comparison, we split the pooled Low-AS and Mid-AS cases into two strata at the median of the common-scale purity and, within each stratum, used a one-sided Brunner–Munzel test to assess whether REO scores tended to be higher in Mid-AS than in Low-AS cases; at these group sizes, the permutation distribution was enumerated exactly. None of these purity analyses was designated as a confirmatory test of an AS-band association independent of purity.

### External gene-list comparison

We cross-referenced two evidence classes. The validated-anchor class comprised the separate tumor and normal lists from Abend et al. (2013), the tumor-minus-normal paired-difference list from Abend et al. (2012), the normal-tissue list from Dom et al. (2012), and CLIP2 as a single-gene tumor reference. The Abend et al. (2012) list had no matching estimand in the present contrasts and was designated indirect. The multivariate-signature class comprised the 50-gene shared-tissue, 45-gene normal-tissue, and 64-gene tumor-tissue transcriptomic signatures in Ory et al. (2026) Supplementary Tables S2, S4, and S6 <!-- N-85 -->. We kept these signatures separate from the validated anchors because Ory et al. selected them using age- and sex-adjusted sPLS-DA or DIABLO rather than gene-wise testing followed by qRT-PCR confirmation.

We mapped published symbols to the GENCODE v36 annotation and, for each source list and contrast, counted k of n genes, where n was the number of listed genes present among the contrast's tested genes and k was the number also present in its q<0.10 list. Entries absent from GENCODE v36 or not retained in a contrast-specific tested set did not contribute to n. The Ory shared-tissue list was compared with both tissues; the normal- and tumor-tissue lists were additionally labeled as tissue-matched or cross-tissue for each contrast. For each overlap, we recorded the current contrast's effect direction and, when the source report supplied a direction, described whether the directions agreed; cross-tissue direction comparisons remained explicitly labeled as such. Because the source studies differed in tissue, platform, contrast, driver control, and list-construction procedure, no enrichment statistic was calculated. We reported every overlap count descriptively and did not use the counts or direction comparisons to change the inferential claims.

## Supplementary Results

### Gene-set analysis and complete-null assessment

After applying the 15–500-gene size filter, 6,141–6,242 sets were tested per contrast across the four collections <!-- N-28 -->. No set met the within-collection Benjamini–Hochberg threshold q<0.10 in any of the 16 contrast-by-collection cells. The smallest adjusted value was q=0.114 in the BRAF-tumor radiation-curated cell (Table S6; Supplementary Data 1) <!-- N-27, N-28 -->.

Across the held-out complete-null assessment, the proportion of replicates producing at least one discovery ranged from 0.01 to 0.18 across the 16 cells, and 13 cells were at or below the nominal 0.10 level <!-- N-24 -->. The BRAF-tumor radiation-curated cell had a rate of 0.10 (descriptive 95% Clopper–Pearson interval, 0.049–0.176), and the BRAF-normal radiation-curated cell had a rate of 0.12 (0.064–0.200) <!-- N-26 -->. The only descriptive interval lying entirely above 0.10 was for BRAF-normal/Hallmark, which had a rate of 0.18 (0.110–0.269) <!-- N-25 -->. Descriptively pooled across cells, 102 of 1,600 replicates produced at least one discovery (0.064) <!-- N-24 -->. Because the 100 pseudo-observations within a cell shared one null pool, their indicators were dependent; the Clopper–Pearson intervals treat them as independent and may therefore be too narrow (Fig. S2; Table S7).

In the single positive-control scenario, 195 genes from HALLMARK_ADIPOGENESIS were multiplied by 1.15 in the nine High-AS BRAF-tumor samples. The planted set had a normalized enrichment score of 2.28, permutation p=0.0002, and q=0.0101, ranking first among 50 Hallmark sets <!-- N-30, N-31 -->. No other Hallmark set met q<0.10 <!-- N-32 -->. This result documents recovery in one coherent-signal configuration and is not a general power analysis.

### Over-representation annotation of the RET-tumor list

The over-representation analysis was a descriptive annotation under a gene-sampling reference, distinct from the subject-label permutation analysis above. Across Hallmark, C2 canonical pathways, C5 GO Biological Process, and the radiation-curated family, respectively, the numbers of sets meeting the family-by-list q<0.10 rule were 0/0/0/0 for genes expressed more highly in High-AS cases, 12/105/205/7 for genes expressed less highly in High-AS cases, and 6/37/71/2 for the combined list (Table S3) <!-- N-59 -->.

The lower-expression list was concentrated in a recurring, overlapping theme involving proliferation, cell cycle, and DNA repair. Illustrative counts were 46 of 199 genes for E2F targets, 41 of 198 for G2M checkpoint <!-- N-61 -->, and 68 of 322 for Reactome DNA repair <!-- N-62 -->. The radiation-curated set with the smallest raw hypergeometric p-value contained 46 of its 126 genes in the lower-expression list, compared with 6.4 expected under the gene-sampling reference <!-- N-60 -->. The radiation-curated findings therefore reflected the same cell-cycle theme rather than an independent line of evidence. These annotations remained hypothesis-generating and did not alter the absence of a q<0.10 result in the label-permutation gene-set analysis.

### Sex-chromosome annotation of the RET-tumor list

Among the 1,765 RET-tumor genes meeting the prespecified Storey q<0.10 rule (Supplementary Data 2), 57 were X-linked: 36 were expressed more highly and 21 less highly in High-AS cases; no Y-linked gene met the rule <!-- N-82 -->. The 15,621 genes tested in the contrast included 572 X-linked and two Y-linked genes <!-- N-82 -->. These counts were descriptive; no enrichment test was performed, and they do not exclude sex-related expression structure.

### REO pair construction

The top-500 construction ranking contained 318 genes expressed more highly and 182 expressed less highly in High-AS RET fusion-positive PTCs <!-- N-35 -->. One higher-expression gene had zero TPM in at least one construction sample and therefore failed the finite-log2-TPM requirement, leaving 317 higher- and 182 lower-expression genes eligible for pairing. Of their 57,694 cross-direction pairs, 153 met all prespecified stability, separation, and reversal criteria <!-- N-35 -->. Across these qualifying pairs, the absolute shift in median within-sample log2-TPM difference ranged from 1.159 to 4.700, and the High-AS reversal rate ranged from 0.53 to 0.87 <!-- N-36 -->. Greedy selection with the prespecified gene-reuse and redundancy restrictions produced the final 10-pair panel. The maximum reversal score among dose-zero construction cases was 2; consequently, scores greater than 2 were classified as positive (Table S4) <!-- N-38 -->.

The construction-band classifications and intermediate-band results are reported in the main text. Their interpretation remains asymmetric: construction-band separation establishes that the selected panel represents its source contrast but is not an unbiased estimate of classification performance; application to Low-AS and Mid-AS cases does not constitute independent validation.

### Ancillary REO quality and purity diagnostics

The ancillary PC-OD screen identified no outliers among the intermediate-band tumors (Low-AS, 0/17; Mid-AS, 0/19) <!-- N-43 -->. Independently of this result, the screen was non-exclusionary, and all 36 tumors remained in the REO application <!-- N-10 -->.

Relative purity could be estimated for 15 Low-AS and 16 Mid-AS tumors <!-- N-44 -->; two Low-AS and three Mid-AS tumors lacked the matched normal assay required by the matched-pair estimator and remained in the REO analysis without a purity score. In the pooled paired RET purity run, median relative purity scores were 0.690, 0.704, 0.739, and 0.814 in the dose-zero, Low-AS, Mid-AS, and High-AS bands, respectively <!-- N-44 -->. This diagnostic refitted the relative scale across all paired RET bands and is therefore not numerically interchangeable with the main-cohort purity scale.

Among the intermediate bands, Spearman correlations between purity and REO score were 0.684 in Low-AS cases, 0.345 in Mid-AS cases, and 0.538 when pooled <!-- N-45 -->. The unadjusted band–score correlation was 0.142, the band–purity correlation was 0.036 <!-- N-48 -->, and the partial Spearman coefficient after adjustment for purity rank was 0.146 (descriptive one-sided permutation p=0.2162) <!-- N-46 -->. Purity-stratified one-sided comparisons of Mid-AS with Low-AS (two strata split at the median purity) gave p=0.3069 (Pr(Low<Mid)=0.580) in the lower-purity stratum and p=0.4197 (Pr(Low<Mid)=0.532) in the higher-purity stratum <!-- N-47 -->.

### External gene-list cross-reference

Among the validated anchors, 19 of 20 source-list-by-contrast cells had no overlap <!-- N-53 -->. The sole overlap was cross-tissue: S100A10 from the Dom et al. normal-tissue list occurred in the RET-tumor q<0.10 list, with expression lower in High-AS cases, opposite to the direction in the original normal-tissue report <!-- N-54 -->.

Five of the 39 Ory shared-tissue signature genes tested in each contrast occurred in the RET-tumor q<0.10 list: ATP5MF, MRPL52, NTHL1, URM1, and USE1 <!-- N-85, N-86 -->. None occurred in RET-normal, BRAF-tumor, or BRAF-normal. Three of 40 tested genes from the Ory normal-tissue signature occurred in RET-tumor—PXDN, S100A10, and TESC<!-- N-86 -->—but none occurred in either normal-tissue contrast or in BRAF-tumor; the RET-tumor overlaps were therefore cross-tissue. Three of 46 tested genes from the Ory tumor-tissue signature occurred in RET-tumor—P2RY1, PLK2, and EHD4<!-- N-86 -->—whereas none of the 48 tested genes occurred in BRAF-tumor. No member of the tumor-tissue signature occurred in either normal-tissue q<0.10 list. These results were descriptive membership counts rather than enrichment tests and did not constitute validation of the multivariate signatures.
