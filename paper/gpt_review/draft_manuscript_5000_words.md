# Driver-conditioned transcriptomic differences across radiation-attributability bands in papillary thyroid carcinoma

> Revised editorial trial prepared on 2026-08-19 from paper/draft_manuscript.md and the canonical ledgers in paper/. It does not supersede those files. Before submission, add the exact GDC accession/data release, ethics statement, and declarations.

## Abstract

### Background

Radiation dose is associated with fusion rather than point-mutation drivers in post-Chernobyl papillary thyroid carcinoma. Because driver composition varies with dose and organizes expression, pooled comparisons can be confounded. We asked whether expression differed by radiation-attributability band within driver strata.

### Methods

We analysed RNA-sequencing counts and clinical data from 440 REBC-THYR cases. Assigned Share (AS) from NIH IREP was used only as an ordinal index. The main cohort comprised 63 paired cases. The primary comparison was High-AS versus dose-zero RET-fusion tumors; normal-tissue and BRAF V600E comparisons were secondary. Brunner–Munzel, Higher Criticism, gene-set, and out-of-sample relative-expression-ordering (REO) analyses used label permutations.

### Results

In RET tumors, 1,765 of 15,621 genes met Storey q<0.10; omnibus p=0.0112. No gene met q<0.10 in RET normal tissue or BRAF tumors. BRAF normal tissue yielded one X-linked gene without corresponding omnibus evidence (p=0.0773). No gene set met q<0.10. The independent REO Mid–Low comparison was inconclusive (one-sided p=0.1127).

### Conclusions

Expression differed between AS bands within RET-fusion tumors, but this does not establish a radiation-specific trace. Age and birth cohort, purity, fusion partner, sex, and unmeasured technical structure remain competing explanations. Normal-tissue and out-of-sample REO evidence was inconclusive.

## Introduction

Papillary thyroid carcinoma after the Chernobyl accident remains a major human model of radiation carcinogenesis. Its molecular hallmark was recognized early: childhood tumors arising after the accident frequently carry RET fusions, and their fusion-partner distribution, with NCOA4-RET predominating, differs from that of age-matched sporadic childhood tumors (Nikiforov et al. 1997; Rabes et al. 2000). The historical labels RET/PTC1 and RET/PTC3 correspond to CCDC6-RET and NCOA4-RET, respectively; we use the gene-partner forms throughout. Experimental irradiation can induce RET rearrangements in thyroid cells (Ito et al. 1993; Caudill et al. 2005), the participating loci lie close together in thyroid-cell nuclei (Nikiforova et al. 2000), and RET rearrangements have also been enriched among highly exposed atomic-bomb survivors (Hamatani et al. 2008). At cohort scale, REBC-THYR showed that increasing thyroid dose was associated with fusion rather than point-mutation drivers (Morton et al. 2021). Whether exposure is also associated with the expression program of a tumor after its driver has been taken into account remains unresolved.

Two features make that question difficult. First, tumor transcriptomes are strongly organized by driver, while driver prevalence itself varies with radiation dose (Morton et al. 2021). A pooled exposed-versus-unexposed analysis can therefore reproduce differences between fusion- and point-mutation-driven tumors rather than an exposure-associated difference within a comparable molecular background. Second, previous expression findings span different biological compartments and designs: dose-associated expression has been reported in tumor and normal tissue, including paired tumor-minus-normal analyses (Abend et al. 2012, 2013); CLIP2 was proposed as a tumor-tissue radiation marker (Hess et al. 2011); and exposed-case normal-tissue signatures have been reported without driver stratification (Dom et al. 2012; Ory et al. 2026). Most of these studies did not condition expression comparisons on driver. The CLIP2 study matched RET-rearrangement status as far as possible, but centered on a copy-number marker and CLIP2 rather than a genome-wide expression comparison within a driver stratum. It is therefore unclear whether previous expression signals persist within the same driver stratum or reflect differences in driver composition.

We hypothesized that a comparison between extreme bands of a case-level radiation-attributability index might reveal structure that an unstratified continuous-dose model could dilute. This choice was motivated by a mixture model in which dose changes the proportion of tumors arising through a radiation-associated initiating event, without requiring expression in every tumor to vary monotonically with dose. Under that model, a continuous-dose regression can attenuate a case-level expression contrast. The index was the Assigned Share (AS) from the NIH Interactive RadioEpidemiological Program, calculated from recorded thyroid dose and age under a fixed convention. In this study AS was used only to order exposed cases and define bands within driver strata. The bands were intended to enrich contrasting groups, not to identify individual tumors as radiogenic or sporadic, and AS was not treated as an observed aetiology or a calibrated individual probability.

Only two molecular strata contained both dose-zero and exposed cases in numbers supporting driver-stratified analysis: RET-fusion tumors and BRAF V600E tumors. Within each stratum, we compared the High-AS band with dose-zero cases separately in tumor tissue and matched normal tissue; these four comparisons were prespecified. The RET-tumor comparison was the single primary comparison because the RET-fusion stratum carried the strongest prior expectation of radiation-related tumor initiation. This hierarchy followed the cohort-level association of increasing dose with fusion rather than point-mutation drivers. BRAF therefore supplied a distinct within-driver question, not a negative or specificity control. The other three comparisons addressed separate secondary questions: whether a corresponding difference was present in normal tissue, whether expression differed in BRAF V600E tumors, and whether normal-tissue patterns were concordant across driver strata. Their results were not combined into a study-wide significance criterion. We specified how each possible pattern would be interpreted before the finalized analysis produced the results reported here (Table 2). Separately, we constructed a relative-expression-ordering (REO) panel from the two RET extreme bands and applied the fixed panel, without refitting, to Low- and Mid-AS RET tumors.

## Methods

### Data sources

Gene-level STAR counts for REBC-THYR were downloaded from the National Cancer Institute (NCI) Genomic Data Commons (GDC) and verified against the download manifest. The clinical source was Data S1 of Morton et al. (2021), containing 440 cases. GDC files were mapped to cases and biospecimens through the GDC API and assembled into a matrix of 58,448 nonzero genes and 906 samples. Strandedness, gene-length derivation, file-selection rules, and the required accession and release identifiers are described in Supplementary Methods.

### Assigned Share

For each exposed case, we calculated the AS associated with the expected excess relative risk using the NIH IREP thyroid model, version 5.7.3, and the recorded thyroid dose and ages (Kocher et al. 2008). The input route followed IREP guidance for internal iodine-131 exposure, and the acute exposure-rate convention followed previous IREP applications to post-Chernobyl papillary carcinoma (Zurnadzhy et al. 2022; Bogdanova et al. 2022). In IREP, exposure rate acts through the dose and dose-rate effectiveness factor; under the acute setting it approaches 1 at and above an uncertain reference dose of 0.03–0.2 Gy, while the minimum thyroid dose in the High-AS band was 188 mGy. A chronic setting would lower the numerical AS scale and could alter band membership. The thyroid risk model was fitted to external photon exposures; applying it to internal iodine-131 is therefore a model-transfer assumption. Dose was entered as a point value, so dosimetric and model uncertainty did not propagate into band membership.

AS was used only as an ordinal design index. Dose-zero cases formed the reference group, labelled Sporadic in the analysis files. Exposed cases were classified as Low (0<AS<33.3), Mid (33.3≤AS<66.6), or High (AS≥66.6); no case lay on a boundary. The four extreme-band contrasts used High-AS versus dose-zero group membership, not the numeric AS value, and AS was not interpreted as a calibrated probability that an individual tumor was radiogenic. The full input convention and the implications of alternative exposure-rate settings are in Supplementary Methods.

### Quality control and cohorts

Eligibility conditions were encoded as case-level flags before the reported inference. Principal-component-scores-based outlier detection (PC-OD) was applied separately within each observed group-by-tissue matrix after expression filtering, using unnormalized log counts per million with prior count 2 and iterative two-sided screening at α=0.05 (Nakayama et al. 2024). PC-OD preceded relative tumor-purity estimation so that an anomalous sample could not affect the purity fit. Purity was estimated from matched tumor-normal counts with the proportion component of contamDE-lm after MUREN normalization (Shen et al. 2016; Ji et al. 2020; Feng and Li 2021). Dose-zero and High-AS cases were estimated together within each driver cohort to place both groups on a common relative scale. Purity served as a within-cohort eligibility filter and diagnostic variable, not as an absolute cellular fraction.

The main cohort comprised RET- or BRAF-classified cases in the dose-zero or High-AS group, with one paired tumor and normal sample, both tissues passing the outlier screen, and relative purity ≥0.6 (n=63). The RET subset was used to train the REO panel. RET tumors in the Low-AS and Mid-AS bands formed the evaluation set and were deliberately not excluded by the training outlier or purity rules (n=36). Their quality metrics were reported without exclusion authority.

### Analysis contrasts and interpretation

Four contrasts were formed by crossing driver stratum with tissue. The analysis-file labels were `R_Tumor` for RET-fusion tumors, `R_Normal` for matched normal tissue from RET-fusion cases, `B_Tumor` for BRAF V600E tumors, and `B_Normal` for matched normal tissue from BRAF V600E cases. These labels denote High-AS versus dose-zero comparisons, not sample groups. Within every contrast, X denoted the dose-zero group and Y the High-AS group. The Brunner–Munzel relative effect, denoted p here and distinguished from a hypothesis-test p-value, was p=P(X<Y)+0.5P(X=Y); values above 0.5 indicated higher expression in High-AS cases.

The RET-tumor Higher Criticism (HC) test was the single primary contrast-level test. The RET-normal contrast asked whether matched normal thyroid carried a corresponding difference. The BRAF-normal contrast supplied the other driver stratum for the prespecified comparison of normal-tissue profiles. The BRAF-tumor contrast completed the driver-stratified tumor analysis but was direction-agnostic and was not treated as a specificity control. Because these secondary contrasts answered distinct questions and were not combined to declare overall study success, no across-contrast adjustment was applied. All four results are reported, and no across-contrast family-wise error rate or study-wide false discovery rate (FDR) is claimed.

Table 2 records the interpretation map assigned before the finalized analysis produced the results reported here. It identified the RET-tumor comparison as the expected signal-bearing contrast, specified the normal-tissue question, and required potential BRAF-normal findings to be read with confounding first. Results outside this map are labelled hypothesis-generating.

### Candidate confounders

Age, sex, and RET fusion partner followed directly from the design and were reported rather than used to redefine eligibility. AS includes age at exposure, and the single 1986 exposure event induces age and birth-cohort structure across bands. We therefore report group distributions and Hodges–Lehmann differences in age at surgery with 95% bootstrap intervals, but do not treat them as tests of confounding. Sex composition was reported because sex-chromosome expression can differ mechanically between groups. RET fusion partners were reported because partner distributions have differed between radiation-associated and sporadic tumors (Nikiforov et al. 1997; Rabes et al. 2000).

We did not combine these variables in a covariate-adjusted inferential model. Age contributes to the definition of AS; relative purity was estimated from the expression data and used for eligibility; and RET fusion partner may be part of the biology connecting exposure history, tumor initiation, and expression. Treating them collectively as nuisance terms would therefore define a different conditional estimand rather than recover the prespecified marginal AS-band contrast. Their distributions are disclosed so readers can assess the observed structure, not to demonstrate absence of confounding.

Residual tumor purity, age and birth cohort, sex, fusion partner, and unmeasured technical structure can still align with the exposure label. The estimates are therefore associations between AS-defined groups. No sequencing- or processing-batch field was available in the analysis objects; this absence is reported as a limitation rather than treated as evidence of balance.

### Normalization and gene-level inference

Protein-coding genes retained by filterByExpr were normalized separately for each contrast with an in-house implementation of three-iteration DEGES-MUREN (Kadota et al. 2012; Sun et al. 2013; Feng and Li 2021). Potential differentially expressed genes were removed from scaling-factor estimation with a permutation Brunner–Munzel screen at Storey q<0.10. Detailed filtering, the floorPDEG rule, convergence, and scaling factors are in Supplementary Methods and Table S2.

For each gene, we enumerated all C(n,nx) allocations of a studentized Brunner–Munzel statistic (two-sided) on the finalized normalized matrix. Enumeration was exhaustive and therefore introduced no Monte Carlo error. The DEGES exclusion screen used the observed group labels, and the resulting normalized matrix was held fixed across enumerated allocations. Inferential interpretation therefore remains conditional on exchangeability of the retained samples, and the enumeration is not an end-to-end randomization from raw counts because group-aware normalization was not repeated within allocations. Gene-level lists used the prespecified Storey q-value rule with a plug-in estimate of the null proportion π0 at λ=0.5 and q<0.10 (Storey 2002). We describe the resulting genes as meeting this rule rather than claiming FDR control under arbitrary gene dependence.

Contrast-level evidence was summarized with a prespecified Higher Criticism statistic scanning the lower 10% of the ordered p-values (Donoho and Jin 2004). Its p-value came from 9,999 label shuffles within the contrast (seed 19860426). Count and maximum statistics and the full rejection curve were retained as descriptive diagnostics. We report these p-values continuously and do not assign a binary label to each contrast.

### Gene-set inference

Genes were ranked by tie-averaged normal scores of the signed Brunner–Munzel statistic. An in-house weighted running-sum statistic was evaluated only at tie-block boundaries and equalled the standard gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) statistic on tie-free input (Subramanian et al. 2005). The same 9,999 label shuffles used at gene level formed the set-level reference. Sign-conditional permutation p-values were adjusted by Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) within each collection, with q<0.10 and no cross-collection claim.

We tested Hallmark, selected C2 canonical-pathway subcollections (Reactome, WikiPathways, KEGG MEDICUS, BioCarta, and PID), C5 GO Biological Process, and a radiation-curated C2:CGP family; sets outside 15–500 genes were excluded. Complete-null operating characteristics were assessed with held-out label shuffles before the reported real-data run, and a one-set 1.15-fold spike-in provided a single-scenario positive-control check. These exercises did not establish FDR control under every partial-alternative configuration. Full procedures are in Supplementary Methods.

As a descriptive complement, the primary RET-tumor q<0.10 list was tested for one-sided hypergeometric over-representation in the same collections, using all genes tested in that contrast as the universe and BH adjustment within family and direction. These gene-sampling p-values do not represent subject-level randomization; experiment-level interpretation rests on the label-permutation analysis (Goeman and Bühlmann 2007).

### Between-stratum concordance

For each tissue, signed gene-level Brunner–Munzel statistics from the RET and BRAF contrasts were compared over shared genes by Spearman correlation. Independently shuffled labels within each stratum supplied a reference interval. The normal-tissue comparison was hypothesis-bearing; the tumor comparison was a descriptive completion. A correlation outside the interval indicated shared label-aligned structure but could not distinguish exposure from a shared covariate.

### REO panel

The REO panel used within-sample gene ordering and therefore required no between-sample normalization (Geman et al. 2004; Wang et al. 2015; Guan et al. 2016). Transcripts per million (TPM) were recalculated from the selected count assay and exon-union gene lengths. Candidate pairs were generated from the 500 RET-tumor genes with the largest Brunner–Munzel effect magnitude, rather than from the q<0.10 list. Prespecified stability, reversal, and redundancy rules selected 10 pairs without gene reuse. A sample's reversal score was the number of pairs, from 0 to 10, that exceeded a dead zone and reversed the dose-zero training sign. The training-derived classification boundary was a score greater than 2.

The fixed panel was applied without alteration to Low-AS and Mid-AS RET tumors. Because both extreme bands determined pair selection and the boundary, their apparent separation describes training fit and is not a performance estimate. The sole prespecified out-of-sample comparison was a one-sided Monte Carlo Brunner–Munzel test of Mid over Low (seed 19860426). For evaluation tumors with matched normal tissue, relative purity was estimated on a common all-band RET scale, and association between band and score conditional on purity was summarized by partial Spearman correlation with a one-sided permutation reference. These ancillary diagnostics did not authorize exclusions or provide independent validation.

### External anchors and reproducibility

Quantitative reverse-transcription PCR (qRT-PCR)-validated gene lists from Abend et al. (2012, 2013), Dom et al. (2012), and CLIP2 were cross-referenced against each contrast's q<0.10 list as descriptive membership counts, without an enrichment test.

The publication run used R 4.5.3 on Ubuntu 24.04 with reference BLAS/LAPACK 3.12.0, four workers, and fixed seeds in a date-pinned container. Two x86-64 machines used md5-identical raw inputs, passed 415 tests with no failures, and produced identical primary artifacts. Analysis code, versioned inputs, and the container recipe accompany the paper; package versions are listed in Table S5.

Generative AI assistants were used under author direction for code debugging and refactoring and for drafting and editing text. The authors specified and reviewed the scientific content, checked quantitative statements against versioned outputs and citations against the source ledger, made all scientific decisions, and accept responsibility for the manuscript.

## Results

### Cohort

Of 440 REBC-THYR cases, 63 met the main cohort definition (Fig. 1; Table 1; Table S1). The RET-fusion stratum contained 12 dose-zero cases (`R_Sporadic`) and 15 High-AS cases (`R_High`); the BRAF V600E stratum contained 27 dose-zero cases (`B_Sporadic`) and nine High-AS cases (`B_High`). Each contributed one tumor and one matched normal sample. Females comprised 10 of 12 dose-zero and 11 of 15 High-AS RET cases, compared with 23 of 27 and four of nine BRAF cases. RET fusion partners were CCDC6-RET in six and seven cases, NCOA4-RET in two and four, and other partners in four and four, respectively. Most attrition resulted from driver classification and restriction to the extreme bands rather than pairing, outlier screening, or purity filtering. PC-OD flagged no RET tumor or normal sample and therefore did not alter the primary RET-tumor cohort. Its only flag among the four target groups was one High-AS BRAF tumor. The separate REO evaluation set comprised 17 Low-AS and 19 Mid-AS RET tumors.

High-AS cases were older at surgery than dose-zero cases by a Hodges–Lehmann estimate of 2.5 years in the RET stratum (95% confidence interval [CI], −1.0 to 6.0) and 8.0 years in the BRAF stratum (3.0 to 12.0). Relative-purity medians in the RET stratum were 0.783 in dose-zero cases and 0.822 in High-AS cases. Sex and fusion-partner distributions are shown in Table 1. These estimates describe group structure and are not tests that confounding is present or absent.

### Gene-level and contrast-level results

In the primary RET-tumor contrast, 1,765 of 15,621 tested genes met the prespecified Storey q<0.10 rule; 971 were expressed more highly and 794 less highly in the High-AS group. The prespecified Higher Criticism omnibus p-value was 0.0112, providing contrast-level evidence of label-aligned expression structure rather than relying only on the size of the gene list (Fig. 2; Table 3; Fig. S1). The list contained 57 X-linked genes among 1,765, compared with 572 X-linked genes among all 15,621 tested genes; no Y-linked gene was selected.

The three secondary contrasts gave different forms of limited evidence. RET-normal had no gene at q<0.10 and HC p=0.3199. BRAF-tumor also had no gene at q<0.10 and HC p=0.1815; under the prespecified interpretation, this absence was not used as a specificity control for the RET-tumor result. BRAF-normal had one gene at q<0.10, the X-linked BHLHB9 (relative effect 0.967, q=0.013), while its contrast-level HC p-value was 0.0773. A descriptive maximum-statistic p-value was 0.0125. Its π0 estimate was 0.727, compared with 0.955 in RET-normal and 0.943 in BRAF-tumor. We report this divergence between gene-level and contrast-level evidence without assigning the contrast a binary label.

### Gene sets and list annotation

No set met q<0.10 in any of the 16 contrast-by-collection cells; the smallest adjusted value was 0.114 in the BRAF-tumor radiation-curated family (Table S6; Supplementary Data 1). In the complete-null assessment, at least one set was selected in 102 of 1,600 held-out contrast-by-collection replicates (0.064 pooled), while BRAF-normal/Hallmark had a cell-specific rate of 0.18 (95% CI 0.110–0.270). These quantities describe operation under complete-null inputs and are not a general FDR guarantee under partial alternatives. In the single positive-control scenario, the planted set ranked first among 50 Hallmark sets (q=0.0101), and no other set met q<0.10 (Fig. S2; Table S7).

Under the separate gene-sampling reference, genes expressed less highly in High-AS RET tumors were over-represented in proliferation, cell-cycle, and DNA-repair annotations, including E2F targets (46/199), G2M checkpoint (41/198), and Reactome DNA repair (68/322). Radiation-curated flags reflected the same cell-cycle theme rather than an independent theme. No family met the over-representation threshold among genes expressed more highly in High-AS RET tumors (Table S3). These annotations were hypothesis-generating and did not change the null result of the label-permutation gene-set analysis.

### Cross-stratum concordance

The RET-normal and BRAF-normal contrast profiles had Spearman rho=0.376 across 15,459 shared genes, within the shuffled reference interval of −0.46 to 0.46 (two-sided p=0.1199). Because the RET-normal contrast itself showed no detectable structure, this prespecified comparison could not distinguish absence of a shared normal-tissue pattern from a shared pattern below the study's detection limit.

The corresponding tumor-profile comparison, included as a descriptive completion rather than a prespecified hypothesis test, gave rho=0.459 across 15,560 genes, outside its shuffled interval of −0.39 to 0.39 (p=0.0197; Table S8). This pattern was compatible with either expression structure shared across driver backgrounds or covariates aligned with both exposure labels; it was therefore treated as hypothesis-generating.

### REO evaluation

Of 57,694 candidate pairs, 153 met the selection criteria and 10 formed the final panel (Table S4). In training, all 12 dose-zero RET cases scored below the classification boundary and 13 of 15 High-AS RET cases scored above it. This separation was expected by construction because both extreme bands informed pair selection and the boundary; it was not treated as an estimate of classification performance.

The fixed score had medians of 0, 1, 4, and 6 across the dose-zero, Low-AS, Mid-AS, and High-AS bands (Fig. 3). In the independent comparison, Mid exceeded Low with effect P(Low<Mid)=0.616 and one-sided p=0.1127. Thus the band ordering was descriptive and the Mid–Low difference remained uncertain.

No evaluation tumor was flagged by the mirrored outlier screen. The matched-pair purity estimator was available for 15 of 17 Low and 16 of 19 Mid cases; the other five cases had a tumor but no matched normal sample. Among the 31 tumors with purity scores, score and purity correlated at rho=0.538; band-score rho was 0.142 and was 0.146 after conditioning on purity (one-sided permutation p=0.2162). Band and purity correlated at rho=0.036. These ancillary diagnostics did not establish an AS-band effect independent of purity.

### External gene lists

Nineteen of 20 external-list-by-contrast cells had no overlap, including every comparison matched on tissue. The only overlap was cross-tissue: S100A10 from the Dom normal-tissue list occurred in the RET-tumor list with the direction opposite to the original report. Because the source studies differed in tissue, platform, contrast, and driver control, these counts were descriptive and did not alter the primary interpretation.

## Discussion

The principal observation was a broad expression difference between High-AS and dose-zero RET-fusion tumors. The prespecified contrast-level omnibus p-value was 0.0112, and 1,765 genes met the prespecified Storey q<0.10 rule. These are complementary rather than interchangeable results: the Higher Criticism test addresses whether the contrast contains label-aligned structure, whereas the q-value rule defines the reported gene list. Together, they support transcriptomic structure aligned with the AS-band labels within the RET-fusion stratum. They do not identify that structure as a radiation-specific molecular trace.

This driver-conditioned question differs from those asked by earlier expression studies. Previous reports used tumor tissue, normal tissue, or paired tumor-minus-normal differences; they also differed in platform, exposure definition, and driver control (Abend et al. 2012, 2013; Hess et al. 2011; Dom et al. 2012; Ory et al. 2026). The absence of tissue-matched overlap with their small validated gene lists is therefore not a direct replication failure, particularly because limited overlap has also been reported among earlier signatures. Conversely, the single cross-tissue, direction-reversed overlap supplies no validation. The present contribution is the within-driver comparison itself, not recovery of an existing universal radiation signature.

The distinction between an AS-band association and a radiation-specific effect is essential because several covariates remain plausible explanations. Exposure status and birth cohort are structurally linked in the source cohort: dose-zero cases were born after the accident, whereas exposed cases were alive in 1986. Age at exposure also contributes to AS. The age-at-surgery difference was smaller in the RET stratum (+2.5 years, with an interval crossing zero) than in the BRAF stratum (+8.0 years), but this does not remove age or the inseparable latency structure as explanations. The RET-tumor list did not show disproportionate sex-chromosome representation, and fusion-partner counts showed no simple gradient across bands; these observations narrow particular explanations without excluding confounding.

Tumor purity also requires care. On the relative scale used for eligibility, median purity was 0.822 in High-AS RET cases and 0.783 in dose-zero RET cases. This modest difference neither establishes purity as the source of the expression pattern nor demonstrates balance between groups. We did not fit a multivariable adjusted model because age contributes to AS, relative purity was expression-derived and used for eligibility, and fusion partner may represent part of the biological pathway under study. Entering them together as nuisance variables would answer a different conditional question rather than deconfound the prespecified marginal AS-band comparison. We therefore disclose the measured structure and leave the effect of these variables unresolved. Technical confounding is also unassessed because the finalized objects contained no sequencing- or processing-batch field.

The BRAF-normal result illustrates why gene-level and contrast-level evidence must remain separate. One X-linked gene met the gene-level rule, but the prespecified omnibus p-value was 0.0773. This contrast also had the largest sex-composition imbalance (23 of 27 dose-zero cases versus four of nine High-AS cases were female) and the largest age difference. In accordance with the interpretation map, we report the evidence continuously and read confounding first rather than labelling the contrast positive. The BRAF-normal/Hallmark cell was also the sole calibration cell with a complete-null selection rate above the nominal level, which further limits any set-level interpretation for this comparison.

At the gene-set level, the label-permutation analysis selected no set. The separate over-representation analysis nevertheless showed that genes expressed less highly in High-AS RET tumors were concentrated in proliferation, cell-cycle, and DNA-repair annotations. The several named sets represent one correlated biological theme, including the radiation-curated flags, rather than independent confirmations. The two procedures also invoke different reference processes: label permutation preserves the subject-level design and gene dependence, whereas over-representation treats the selected genes against a gene-sampling universe. Their disagreement is therefore about inferential evidence, not necessarily about the ordering of biological themes. The annotation is a lead for mechanistic work, but no primary claim rests on it.

The two cross-stratum comparisons had different roles. Similarity between the RET-normal and BRAF-normal profiles was the prespecified test of a possible shared normal-tissue pattern. Its result was indeterminate: the observed correlation lay within the shuffled reference interval, and RET-normal itself showed no detectable structure. The data therefore cannot distinguish absent sharing from sharing below the detection limit. The tumor profiles were more concordant than their shuffled reference, but that comparison was a descriptive completion. It is compatible both with expression structure shared across driver backgrounds and with covariates aligned in both tumor contrasts, so it remains hypothesis-generating.

The REO panel translated the RET extreme-band difference into a per-case score, but separation of those same extreme bands is training fit by construction. Low- and Mid-AS RET tumors provided the only out-of-sample comparison. Their effect estimate was in the prespecified direction, P(Low<Mid)=0.616, but the one-sided p-value was 0.1127. The four-band medians are consequently a descriptive ordering rather than evidence of classification performance or a dose-response relation. Correlation between score and purity among the 31 evaluation tumors with purity estimates adds uncertainty. The panel should not be considered a classifier or radiation-attributability marker without independent validation using fixed gene pairs and complete purity and technical metadata.

Several limitations cap interpretation and generalizability. The study is cross-sectional within driver-defined strata and cannot distinguish a radiation-related process shared across tumor initiation from an association between expression pattern and the type of initiating lesion. The High-AS BRAF group contained only nine cases, and the absence of genes meeting the reporting rule in a secondary contrast should not be read as evidence of equivalence between its AS bands. The 1,765-gene list depends on the specified Storey plug-in estimator, for which we do not claim FDR control under arbitrary transcriptomic dependence. PC-OD was an upstream QC step and removed no RET sample; it was not part of the gene-level hypothesis test. Exhaustive Brunner–Munzel enumeration removed Monte Carlo error on the finalized normalized matrix, but inferential validity still depends on exchangeability, and group-aware DEGES normalization was not repeated within allocations. The procedure is therefore not an end-to-end exact randomization of the raw-data pipeline. No wet-laboratory or independent cohort replication was available, the purity measure was relative within cohort, and IREP transferred an external-photon thyroid model to internal iodine-131 exposure.

In conclusion, High-AS and dose-zero RET-fusion tumors differed broadly in expression under the reported analysis, whereas evidence from normal tissue, BRAF V600E tumors, label-permutation gene sets, and the independent REO comparison was limited or inconclusive. The result motivates further study of driver-conditioned radiation-associated expression, but it should presently be described as an AS-band association. Independent, covariate-informed validation is needed to determine whether it represents radiation-related biology.
